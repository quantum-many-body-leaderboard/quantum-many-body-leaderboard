                  The two-dimensional Hubbard model was originally proposed as the minimal model of uniform
                d-wave superconductivity in the cuprate high-temperature superconductors, but recent calculations
                with relevant parameters in a realistic doping regime have often found other ground states. Further,
                uncertainty  remains  over  such  results  due  to  the  intrinsic  bias  of  different  computational  meth-
                ods.  Here we address this uncertainty through state-of-the-art neural tensor network and neural
                backflow state simulations,  enabled by large-scale GPU computation,  that together reach an un-
                precedented  variational  accuracy  on  lattices  with  up  to  1024  sites.   At  1/8  doping  in  the  pure
                Hubbard model, as well as with next-nearest-neighbor hopping relevant to the hole-doped cuprates,
                we reveal a near degeneracy among a low-energy uniformd-wave state, superconducting stripes, and
                non-superconducting stripe states on the milli-tenergy scale.  Our work rebalances the narrative
                that the Hubbard model supports a single dominant order in relevant parameter regimes towards a
                picture of extreme competition between stripes and superconductivity.




                                                           I.    INTRODUCTION

  The Hubbard model is a paradigmatic model of strongly correlated electrons [1,2]. Despite its simple form, the two-
dimensional (2D) Hubbard model is believed to capture the essential physics of high-temperature superconductivity
(SC) [3,4], and can be realized directly by ultracold fermionic atoms in optical lattices [5–8]. In the cuprate materials
that the Hubbard model aims to describe,  uniformd-wave superconductivity is found at optimal doping,  while in
certain cuprates other low-temperature orders can also be observed [9,10].  For example, there is evidence for various
unidirectional orders such as spin-density waves (SDW), charge-density waves (CDW), stripes (with intertwined spin
and charge modulations), and even more exotic orders such as pair-density waves in several materials at low doping [9,
10], and these have been argued to either coexist with, be intertwined with, or compete with superconductivity.
  Numerical studies of the hole-doped Hubbard model find different ordered ground states at the same point in the
phase diagram.  We summarize some recent results here, which can be roughly categorized as shown in Fig.1(a).  For
the pure Hubbard model, multiple methods such as the density matrix renormalization group (DMRG), projected
entangled pair states (PEPS), density matrix embedding theory (DMET), constrained-path (CP-) and variational (V)
auxiliary-field quantum Monte Carlo (AFQMC), and neural network backflow (NNBF) states all find a filled stripe at
U= 8 and 1/8 doping [11–15], while Ref. [13] also saw an energetically competitive uniformd-wave superconducting
state at that point.  With a next-nearest-neighbor hoppingt            ′ ≈ −0.2 relevant to the hole-doped cuprates [16,17], the
conclusions are more varied. Again at 1/8 doping, robust superconductivity coexisting with partially filled stripes has
been reported in a DMRG/CP-AFQMC study [18] and a neural Pfaffian study [15], whereas other simulations using
infinite PEPS [19] and other NNBF states [20,21] have found non-superconducting stripes,  while a DMRG study
found only a CDW with short-range pairing [22].
  The diversity of observations can be traced to the sensitivity of the Hubbard model ground state to biases of the
numerical  simulations.   While  it  is  possible  to  make  progress  through  the  consensus  of  different  techniques  which
individually have uncertainties larger than the energy scale of competing orders—for example, the study of stripes
in Ref. [11] involved an energy spread across methods of roughly 0.01t, larger than the energy difference between
competing stripe wavelengths—the only way to eliminate this uncertainty is to improve the absolute accuracy of the
simulations. To this end, we employ variational wavefunctions that can in principle be improved to arbitrary accuracy
and that in practice we push to the precision of a few milli-ton lattices with up to 1024 sites. We employ two variants
of NNBF [23], which have recently demonstrated high accuracy in Hubbard model studies [15,20,21,24–26], as well
as a newly introduced neural tensor network state [27], namely the neural-network fermionic PEPS (NN-fPEPS). The

  The  tensor  contraction  in  Eq. (8)  is  performed  approximately  by  the  higher-order  tensor  renormalization  group
(HOTRG) [44,45], which repeatedly merges pairs of adjacent rows, compresses the doubled bonds back to dimension

contraction path(s) for every configurationn, the approximate HOTRG contraction ensures strict variationality [46]
for anyD, χ,  and can be easily parallelized [47].  Its time complexity per amplitude isO(Nχ                    7),  orO(N) at fixed
Dandχ.  To avoid the high cost of largeDandχ,  we chooseD=χ= 4 throughout this work and rely on the
neural network rather than the tensor network to increase the expressivity.  To further restore the strict translation
symmetry [26] broken by HOTRG, we expressψ                     NN-fPEPS(n) as a superposition of a set of contraction paths, which
leads to a slightly higher computational complexity ofO(NlogN) (SM Sec. III [34]).  The NN-fPEPS thus avoids the
O(N      3) cost of the Pfaffian and the determinant in the backflow calculations at large system sizes.
  The NN-fPEPS and the backflow wavefunctions retain different inductive biases arising from how the fermionic
sign structure is enforced, as illustrated in Fig.1(b).  The BCS and Pfaffian backflow states capture pairing through
the mean-field wavefunction and have been shown to reach high accuracy if the ground state is indeed a BCS su-
perconductor  [24, 26].   Empirically,  however,  it  has  been  argued  that  these  reference  forms  bias  the  state  toward
superconductivity [29].  In addition, Sec.IVincludes the determinant backflow state (ACE) of Ref. [21], which poten-
tially favors stripes over superconductivity [29].  The fPEPS in the NN-fPEPS, in contrast to backflow wavefunctions,
favors locally entangled (area-law) ground states [48,49].  A priori, this feature favors neither stripes nor supercon-
ductivity, although specific stripe or superconducting states may carry different amounts of entanglement.
  Our simulations are implemented inQuantax[50], which is built onJax[51] andEquinox[52], and the fPEPS
contraction relies onquimb[53, 54] andsymmray[33, 55].  In Fig.2(b),  we illustrate the techniques used in our
VMC optimization.  All parts of the computational graph have been intensively optimized to achieve high throughput
on GPUs, including custom kernels for the HOTRG contraction and for the batched determinants and Pfaffians [56].
Several symmetries, including mirror reflections and spin inversion, are imposed by a symmetry projection for better
accuracy (SM Sec. III [34]).  During the VMC optimization, the variational parameters are trained by an adaptive
moment  stochastic  reconfiguration  (AdamSR)  optimizer,  which  combines  Adam  [57]  with  common  optimizers  for
NQS [20,58–62] for better efficiency and stability.  Apart from the common Cholesky solver, we further utilize the
iterative least-squares minimal residual (LSMR) linear solver [63] in AdamSR to achieve efficient parallelization on up
to 1024 GPUs.  The optimizers and solvers are detailed in SM Sec. IV [34], and a careful analysis of the time cost is
included in SM Sec. V [34]. Unless otherwise specified, all simulations are performed with 32-bit floating-point (FP32)
precision and TensorFloat-32 (TF32) tensor cores for convolutions and matrix multiplications.



                                                                 IV.    RESULTS

  We now compare the results of the three classes of wavefunctions,  namely the BCS backflow state,  the Pfaffian
backflow state, and the NN-fPEPS, at 1/8 doping andU= 8, first att               ′ = 0 and then att   ′ =−0.2.  The variational
energies and variances of all our states before and after symmetry projection, together with those of previous studies,
are listed in SM Sec. VII [34]. The real-space spin, charge, and pair-pair correlation functions of all symmetry-projected
states are shown in SM Sec. VIII [34].



                                                         A.    Pure Hubbard model

  We begin with the pure Hubbard model att   ′ = 0 and 1/8 doping, where the physics has been more extensively
studied  than  att   ′  =−0.2.   As  presented  in  Fig.3(a),  we  reach  the  lowest  variational  energies  reported  so  far  on
both the 16×16 and 32×32 PBC lattices,  below those of the convolutional determinant backflow forms used in
ACE and SCALE [21], of the hidden fermion Pfaffian state (HFPS) [15], and of the VAFQMC stripe state [13,64]
listed in SM Sec. VII [34].  On the 16×16 lattice, extrapolating a pure fPEPS simple-update calculation with PBC
gives an estimated exact energy ofE/N=−0.7602(14) (SM Sec. VI [34]),  shown in Fig.3(a).  The energies of all
our variational wavefunctions lie within 0.002tper site (0.3%) of this estimate, and the lowest one (BCS backflow)
lies 0.0014tabove it.  This accuracy is consistent with that of the NN-fPEPS on the small 16×4 lattice, where an
accurate benchmark energy is known (SM Sec. VII [34]).

long-ranged-wave pairing.  The ansatzes differ even more in the strength of their stripe order, shown in Fig.4(c).

M 2str = 0.0125 and 0.0030, the latter being already 3.3 times weaker than the 0.00998(1) order of the Pfaffian backflow
on the same lattice.  Its peakS(k           α) hardly changes between the two sizes, so the spin correlations are short-ranged,
and the extrapolation in 1/L  2  givesM    2str =−0.0001.  We note that our extrapolation, which starts from the small
12×12 lattice, is subject to strong finite-size effects and may thus not be entirely reliable.  Nevertheless, since the
stripe order is much weaker than that of the other two variational states, we regard the BCS backflow state as evidence
of a (nearly) uniform low-energy state in the TDL. The Pfaffian backflow state and the NN-fPEPS instead form a
pronounced stripe order withq=π/4, i.e., a spin period of 8, whoseM                              2str extrapolates to 0.0100 for the NN-fPEPS
and 0.0070 for the Pfaffian backflow, indicating a striped state in the TDL. Similarly, the determinant backflow state
also shows a strong stripe order withq=π/4 [21].  Independent of this consensus between these different ansatzes,
the low energy achieved by the Pfaffian backflow wavefunction lends further support to a non-uniform ground state
in the TDL.
   The extrapolation of the superconducting pairing order is shown in Fig.4(c).  Extrapolating ∆          2SC, we find that the
BCS backflow state of Ref. [26] retains a sizable ∆      SC = 0.019(4) in the TDL, slightly below the value 0.022(1) obtained
in  Ref.  [26]  by  extrapolating  ∆      SC  itself.   Since,  as  discussed  above,  its  stripe  order  is  very  weak  (and  potentially
vanishes  in  a  TDL  extrapolation),  we  reinterpret  this  as  evidence  of  a  (nearly)  uniformd-wave  superconducting
low-energy state in the TDL, rather than the striped superconducting state originally discussed in Ref. [26].  The
Pfaffian  backflow  state  and  the  NN-fPEPS  give  much  weaker  pairing  in  the  TDL.  For  the  NN-fPEPS,  the  linear
                                                                                                                                                  8

fit through the 16×16 and 32×32 lattices yields ∆                 SC  = 0.007(2).  For the Pfaffian backflow, the result depends
on the fit.  The linear 1/Lfit overL= 16,  24,  and 32 in Fig.4(c) gives ∆                         SC  consistent with zero,  with an upper
bound of 0.006 at one standard deviation.  A fit through the 24×24 and 32×32 lattices alone gives a small but
finite ∆SC  =  0.010(3).   Either way,  the extrapolated pairing gives ∆    SC ≲0.010,  i.e.,  a striped ground state with
weak or absent superconductivity.  This stands in contrast to the striped superconducting ground state with a longer
wavelength and a larger ∆SC = 0.017(3) proposed by CP-AFQMC [18].  Taken together, these results thus point to
stripes with weaker or absent superconductivity, stripes with robust superconductivity, and a uniformd-wave state,
all existing in competition near the ground state.


                                               V.    CONCLUSION AND OUTLOOK

  We have studied the competition between stripes and superconductivity in the hole-doped Hubbard model with
U= 8 and 1/8 doping using three classes of variational states:  BCS backflow states, Pfaffian backflow states, and
NN-fPEPS. On the lattices considered,  from 16×16 up to 32×32 sites,  the accuracy of our variational energies
exceeds that of all prior studies of this model. In the pure Hubbard model att   ′ = 0, our work lends further support to
the long-standing proposal of a non-superconducting striped ground state [11,12].  Att           ′ =−0.2, we find evidence for
weak or vanishing superconductivity with strong stripe order in the ground state, which is different from recent studies
that saw a robustd-wave striped superconducting ground state [15,18], although the latter could still represent a
different low-lying state [11].   Both with and withoutt                 ′,  we find at this doping that an ordered stripe suppresses
superconductivity, so that the ground state is either not superconducting or only weakly superconducting.  However,
at both values oft   ′, we also find a strongly superconducting uniformd-wave state nearby on a milli-tenergy scale,
resembling the proposal in Ref. [13].
  Beyond the physical findings,  our work highlights and advances two aspects of current simulation methodology.
First, despite being highly parametrized, modern neural wavefunctions retain biases that are not overcome in training.
They must therefore be cross-validated using different techniques, as done here, in order to identify the qualitative
nature of the ground state.  Second,  the neural tensor networks,  improved here from Ref. [27],  now offer state-of-
the-art performance in large-scale simulations of a strongly correlated electron system,  without using a mean-field
reference.   Taken  together,  these  two  aspects  suggest  a  new  framework  where  neural  wavefunctions  with  different
fermionic references are combined for reliable studies.
  We now discuss prospects for future definitive simulations of superconductivity in the Hubbard model ground state.
A finite lattice can resolve the pairing only once the gap ∆ exceeds the mean spacing of the single-particle levels,
roughly the bandwidth 8tof the square lattice divided byN, which requiresN≳8t/∆.  For ∆                                        SC ∼0.02 comparable
to the uniformd-wave superconductors in this work and previous estimates att                 ′ =−0.2 [18,26], and taking ∆≈∆                              SCt
from BCS theory, eliminating the finite-size effects on the pairing requiresN≳400 sites, a number that is expected
to be further reduced in the strong-coupling limit [67,68].  Running on up to 1024 GPUs, we simulatedN≈1000
sites  and  thus  have  reached  a  regime  where  finite-size  errors  arguably  no  longer  dominate  the  physics  and  TDL
extrapolation is reliable.  On the other hand, the condensation energy relative to a hypothetical paramagnetic normal
state is estimated to be<10    −3   t[69].  If the mechanism driving superconductivity is related to the formation of the
striped state,  we may expect a competing stripe state to gain some of this energy,  and thus to compete with the
superconducting state at the milli-tscale or below, which is what we see in our work.  The overall picture is that
stripes, striped superconductivity, and uniform superconductivity all exist in extreme competition in the hole-doped
Hubbard model, and resolving this competition requires an extreme level of accuracy.  Our work presents a concrete
step in this direction.

   Both the neural-network fermionic projected entangled pair state (NN-fPEPS) and the Bardeen–Cooper–Schrieffer

residual block,  which is the bottleneck block in the NN-fPEPS and the normal block in the backflows.   Since the
convolutions share their weights across sites, the same network applies to every system size, enabling weight transfer
from small to large lattices.

parallelize across devices with modest communication. In this work, we therefore use LSMR for the large-scale parallel
simulations of the NN-fPEPS and the Cholesky solver in all other cases.

                                                    VI.    PURE FPEPS REFERENCE

   We estimate the exact ground-state energy of the 16×16 PBC lattice att          ′  = 0 with pure fPEPS optimized by
simple update (SU) [86,87], evaluated by contracting finite clusters embedded in a belief-propagation environment,
and extrapolated to infinite bond dimension.



                                               A.    Simple update and cluster contraction

   The fPEPS use the local fermionic encoding [32,33,88] withU(1) symmetry [89–91].  The initial product state is a
period-8 stripe, with antiferromagnetic order on columns 1–7 and 9–15, aπphase shift between the two domains, and
empty columns 8 and 16 that hold the 32 holes of 1/8 doping.  We first converge SU at bond dimensionD= 6 and
then continue from this state directly at each target bond dimensionD= 8,10,12,14,16.  All runs use a second-order
                                                                                                                                                     26

Trotter decomposition with the imaginary time stepτ= 0.5D             −3/2     .  All calculations are performed on CPUs with
quimb[53] and thesymmrayarray backend [55].
  To evaluate observables of the SU states on the torus, we first gauge each state by running SU without applying
any  gates,  which  is  equivalent  to  belief  propagation  (BP)  [92, 93].   This  yields  a  bond  weightλ             b  on  each  of  the
Nb = 2L  2 = 512 bonds, shown in Fig.S8(a)–(b).
  For each nearest-neighbor term, acting on sites (i, j) and (i, j+ 1) or (i, j) and (i+ 1, j), we build a rectangular
cluster that extendsrsites in every direction from the two target sites.  A horizontal term thus has aC                  x ×C   y = 4×3
cluster atr= 1 and a 16×15 cluster, which covers nearly the whole lattice, at the largest radiusr= 7.  We cut the
bonds along the cluster boundary and place the weightsλ   b on every bond of the cluster, including the dangling bonds
at its edges.  When the cluster spans the full lattice in one direction,C x = 16 orC    y  = 16, its two far edges meet,
and both ends of the cut bond between them carryλ          b.  This amounts to approximating the environment beyond the
cluster by its BP form.  This process is shown in Fig.S8(b)–(d).
  Each cluster is a PEPS with open boundary conditions, which we partially trace by boundary contraction to obtain
an approximate reduced density matrix of the two target sites, as shown in Fig.S8(e). The two boundary environments
start from the cluster edges parallel to the target bond and sweep inwards, with the ket and bra layers absorbed in
two separate steps and the environments compressed to bond dimensionχafter each step. Once the two environments
are adjacent to the target site column, one environment is compressed with the ket tensors, and the other with the
bra tensors.  See Fig.S8(f)–(i).  The compression is a variant of successive deterministic compression [94].  A sketching
sweep with randomized low-rank SVDs first compresses the environment to the oversampled bond dimension 1.5χ,
truncating every charge sector in proportion, and a final direct SVD then truncates it toχby ranking the singular
values across all charge sectors.  The contraction thus costsO(D                3χ3), remains accurate, and is compatible withU(1)
fermionic tensor networks.  Each reduced density matrix is normalized individually.
  The results for the energy per site,E/N, as a function ofD, r, andχare shown in Fig.S9.  We also show the
energies calculated by full boundary contraction, i.e., without the cluster approximation. These are more expensive to
compute, with a cost ofO(D         5χ3), and converge more noisily, so we do not use them to estimate energies. Nonetheless,
they appear to be consistent with the cluster boundary approximation.

                                                      A.    Zero-variance extrapolation

accounts for the statistical errors of both (Fig.S11(a)).  Repeating the fit over a grid of step windows and bin sizes
