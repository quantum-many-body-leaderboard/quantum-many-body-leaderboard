Abstract
The  pursuit  of  geometrically  frustrated  lattices  beyond  conventional  paradigms  remains  a
central challenge in the design of quantum materials. Herein, we report the discovery of the
A3V9Te13 (A = Cs, Rb) family of vanadium-based intermetallic compounds, which host a unique
two-dimensional Mosaic lattice derived from the Kagome network, composed of an ordered
tessellation  of  triangles,  squares,  and  pentagons.  The  Cs  compound  (CVT)  exhibits  strong
electronic  correlations,  characterized  by  non-Fermi  liquid  behavior  at  low  temperatures,  an
exceptionally  large  Sommerfeld  coefficient,  and  a  bulk  phase  transition  at T*                ≈  47  K   with
possible charge- or spin-related origin. Inspired by pressure-tuning in related Kagome systems,
we demonstrate that the electronic ground state of this lattice is exquisitely tunable via chemical
pressure. Systematic substitution of Cs with smaller Rb ions suppresses the ܶ∗phase transition

and the correlated electronic response, ultimately driving the system into a highly frustrated
semiconducting ground state without long-range magnetic order down to 60 mK. This work
unveils a new structural platform for exploring the interplay between geometric frustration and
strong  electron  correlations,      providing  a  chemically  controllable  platform  for  exploring  the
phase space between distinct correlated electronic states.

1. Introduction
      The  interplay  between  lattice  geometry  and  electronic  correlations  is  a  cornerstone  of
modern  condensed  matter  physics.  Archimedean  lattices1,2,  such  as  triangular,  square,  and

honeycomb networks, have long served as fundamental playgrounds for emerging phenomena
ranging  from  high-Tc       superconductivity3,4      to  Dirac  fermions5,6.  Among  these,  the  Kagome

lattice has recently ascended to prominence. Its geometry of corner-sharing triangles naturally
combines  geometric  frustration7,  non-trivial  band  topology8,9,  and  flat  electronic  bands10,

providing fertile ground for exotic quantum states. Recent discoveries of AV3Sb5 (A = K, Rb,
Cs)  family  have  substantiated  this  potential,  revealing  unconventional  charge  density  wave
(CDW), potential time-reversal symmetry breaking (TRSB), pair density wave (PDW), and
superconductivity8,11–14.  In  the  related  magnetic  compound  CsCr3Sb5,  pressure  suppresses

density-wave order and induces superconductivity, accompanied by quantum critical behavior
and   enhanced   quasiparticle   mass   renormalization15,   underscoring   the   intimate   coupling

between lattice geometry and electronic correlations. These advances inspire us to design novel
Kagome-derived lattices via anion valence engineering, to explore geometric structures and
correlated physics beyond conventional Archimedean tiling.


                                                          2

      Despite  these  advances,  the  structural  landscape  of  two-dimensional  periodic  lattices
extends far beyond conventional Archimedean tiling. Regular pentagons cannot tile the plane
without  distortion16,17,  imposing  fundamental  geometric  constraints  on  periodic  crystalline

frameworks.  Ordered  two-dimensional  periodic  lattices  incorporating  pentagonal  motifs
therefore remain exceptionally rare, and their influence on correlated electronic states is largely
unexplored.
      In this work, we report the discovery of the A3V9Te13 (A = Cs, Rb) family of vanadium-
based intermetallic compounds, in which the two-dimensional vanadium layer forms an ordered
tessellation of triangles, squares, and pentagons. We define this unique geometry as a “Kagome-
derived Mosaic lattice”. This architecture introduces a pentagon-integrated periodic framework
that is structurally related to, yet distinct from, the Kagome network, thereby expanding the
geometric design space of frustrated lattices. Cs3V9Te13 (CVT) exhibits a phase transition of
possible charge or spin origin at T* ≈ 47 K, non-Fermi liquid resistivity behavior, and a large
Sommerfeld coefficient, placing it among strongly correlated electron systems. The correlated
ground state is exquisitely tunable via chemical pressure: systematic substitution of Cs with
smaller  Rb  ions  completely  suppresses  the  electronic  ordering  transition  and  quenches  the
heavy quasiparticle response. Upon full suppression of the ordered state, the system evolves
into a non-magnetic, semiconducting state with extreme magnetic frustration and no long-range
magnetic order down to 60 mK. These results position the A3V9Te13 Mosaic lattice as a versatile
structural platform for realizing and tuning strong electronic correlations and quantum disorder
in a purely d-electron environment.
2. Results and Discussion

of P-62m (No.189). Detailed crystallographic details are provided in Table S1-2 and Figure

      The geometry of a Mosaic lattice is fundamentally entwined with its electronic ground
states. To elucidate the origin of this complex tiling, it is instructive to compare it with the
prototypical  Kagome  lattice  in           AV3Sb5.  Our  Mosaic  lattice  could  be  described  from  a
transformation product of an ideal Kagome lattice (Figure 2): first, atoms in the ideal Kagome
lattice can be divided into two groups (blue triangular and the remaining red vanadium atoms)
to create a √3    ×  √3 supercell (Step 1), and then all blue triangles are rotated by 60° (clockwise
or  counterclockwise)  to  generate  a  highly  symmetric  Mosaic  lattice  (Step  2).  After  full
structural relaxation, the resulting geometry reproduces the Mosaic lattice observed in CVT
(Step 3). Notably, this resulting Mosaic lattice retains the intrinsic geometric frustration of the
parent  Kagome  network  while  introducing  a  higher  degree  of  topological  complexity.  This
structural metamorphosis not only expands the crystal unit cell, but also introduces two distinct
V   sites   with   drastically   different   V-V   bond   lengths,   which   goes   beyond   a   simple
renormalization  of  hopping  parameters  and                significantly  modify  the        electronic  structure,
providing a fertile platform for the strong electronic correlations and tunable quantum phases
discussed hereafter.

      Electronic structure calculations provide crucial insight into the properties of CVT (Figure
4).  Band  structure  and  density  of  states  (DOS)  calculations  were  performed  within  an
antiferromagnetic    framework,    with    ferromagnetic    alignment    in    the                  ab    plane    and
antiferromagnetic  coupling  along  the  out-of-plane  direction.  The  calculations  yield  local
magnetic moments of ~0.1B for V1 and ~1.57 B for V2. The negligible local moment on V1

environments, and further support the mixed-valence character of the V2 sublattice, as implied
by XPS analysis. Meanwhile, the electronic structure exhibits an overall metallic character, with
multiple bands crossing the Fermi level. States near the Fermi level are predominantly derived
from V 3d orbitals. The band structure hosts Dirac points near the K points of the Brillouin
zone, van Hove singularities (vHSs) at the M points, and flat-band features within the Γ-M-K
plane.  These  topological  features  are  analogous  to  those  recently  reported  in  conventional
Kagome superconductors AV3Sb525,26and CsCr3Sb515, and the Kagome metal ScV6Sn627. The

2.3 Chemical Modulation
      The  electronic  ground  states  of  Kagome-related  materials  often  exhibit  a  remarkable
sensitivity  to  lattice  parameters.  A  prominent  example  is  the  CsCr3Sb5                 family,  where  the
application of physical pressure effectively tunes the system from a non-superconducting state
into  a  superconducting  one15.  Inspired  by  such  pressure-induced  modulation,  we  employed
chemical pressure to modulate the Mosaic lattice by substituting the Cs+ ions in CVT with
smaller  Rb+    ions  (Figure  5),     thereby  exploring  the  evolution  of  the  correlated  state  under

structural compression. This ionic substitution leads to a dramatic contraction of the interlayer
spacing,  with  the ܿ-axis  shrinking  from  8.21  Å  to  7.93  Å  (Figure  5a).  Concomitantly,  the
stacking sequence evolves from an A-A-A pattern (space group P-62m) to a staggered A-B-A-
B configuration (Cmcm). Although the vanadium layers retain the same Mosaic lattice, on a
local  scale  the  vanadium  trimers  lose  their  equilateral  symmetry  and  undergo  significant
distortion, as evidenced by the inequivalent V-V bond lengths in the Rb-analogue (Figure 5b).
Detailed  crystallographic  details  are  provided  in          Table  S3-4     and   Figure  S9-10.      The  lattice
contraction  induced  by  Rb  substitution  serves  as  an  effective  chemical  pressure,  which  is

expected  to  modify  the  V  3d  orbital  overlap  and  Te-mediated  superexchange  interactions,
thereby providing a route to tune the correlated electronic state in this system.

2.4 Physical Properties of Rb3V9Te13
The  physical  properties  of          Rb3V9Te13(RVT)           diverge  sharply  from  those  of           CVT.  In  stark
contrast  to  the  metallic  CVT,  RVT  exhibits  semiconducting  behavior  with  a  substantial
activation  energy       Ea   ≈  15   meV  (Figure  5c),  indicating  a  transition  toward  more  localized

                                                              10

electronic states. The magnetic susceptibility χ(T) (Figure 5d) reveals no sharp phase transition
down to 1.8 K. The Curie-Weiss fit yields negative Weiss temperatures θab = -136 K and θc = -
62  K,  with  effective  moments        eff =  0.70   B/V.  and  0.52 B/V        for  the  ab-plane  and c-axis
directions,  respectively.      These  values  indicate  dominant  antiferromagnetic  interactions  and
intrinsic magnetic frustration. At low temperature, the χ(T) curve exhibits a broad shoulder near
~4 K that is gradually suppressed by increasing magnetic field, indicating the emergence of
short-range spin correlations (Figure S11a). Consistently, nonlinear M(H) behavior is observed
at 1.8 K, whereas at 100 K, linear M(H) behavior without saturation up to 9 T is observed, and
no hysteresis is detected at any temperature (Figure S11b). The absence of long-range order is
further confirmed by heat capacity measurements (Figure 5e), which show no ߣ-like anomaly
down to 60 mK. Suppose the antiferromagnetic transition occurs below the measurement limit;
the frustration parameter, f = |θ|/TN, can be estimated to be on the order of 103, suggesting a

highly frustrated system. Such features are characteristic of geometrically frustrated lattices,
where competing interactions suppress long-range ordering and stabilize persistent short-range
spin  correlations28,29.    Notably,     C/T   rapidly  approaches  zero  at  low  temperatures,  in  stark

contrast to the large electronic specific heat coefficient observed in CVT (the upturn below 0.2
K  is  attributed  to  a  nuclear  Schottky  anomaly). Collectively,  these  results  demonstrate  that
chemical   pressure   induced   by   Rb   substitution   contracts   the   interlayer   lattice   spacing,
significantly suppresses the itinerant correlated quasiparticle state of the Mosaic lattice, and
ultimately  drives  the  system  into  a  highly  frustrated  magnetic  semiconducting  ground  state
without long-range magnetic order down to 60 mK.

3. Conclusion and Outlook
      In  summary,  we  have  synthesized  and  characterized  a  new  family  of  vanadium-based
compounds, A3V9Te13 (A = Cs, Rb), which features a unique Kagome-derived Mosaic lattice
geometry  constructed  from  an  ordered  tessellation  of  triangles,  squares,  and  pentagonal
structural  units.  Our  investigation  reveals  that  Cs3V9Te13               hosts  a  prominent  bulk  phase
transition at T* ≈ 47 K, characterized by an itinerant-to-correlated crossover and substantial
Fermi  surface  reconstruction,  likely  associated  with  a  charge-                 or  spin-related  electronic
instability. This transition is accompanied by pronounced quasiparticle mass renormalization,
as  indicated  by  the  exceptionally  large  Sommerfeld  coefficient,  uncovering  extraordinarily
strong electronic correlations in this purely d-electron system. The enhanced effective magnetic
moment  in  the  Cs  system,  compared             with  the  nearly  nonmagnetic  conventional  Kagome
AV3Sb5 compounds, further highlights the robust correlated electronic nature inherent to the
Mosaic framework.
                                                          11

      Furthermore, we demonstrate that the electronic properties of this Kagome-derived Mosaic
lattice  are  highly  tunable  via  chemical  pressure.  Substituting  Cs  with  Rb  systematically
suppresses the T* transition and quenches the strongly correlated quasiparticle response. Upon
complete suppression of the electronic ordered state, the system evolves into a semiconducting
ground   state   without   long-range   magnetic   order   but   with   dominant   antiferromagnetic
interactions.  The  Rb  analogue  exhibits  no  long-range  magnetic  order  down  to  60  mK.
Collectively,  the    A3V9Te13     family  provides  a  versatile  platform  for  exploring  the  interplay
between geometric frustration and strong electron correlations.
      The discovery of the A3V9Te13 family opens several promising avenues for future research.
A key priority among these is to further clarify whether the T* ≈ 47 K transition arises from
electronic  or  spin  correlations       in  Cs3V9Te13,  for  which  variable-temperature  transmission
electron microscopy  (TEM), Scanning Tunneling Microscope (STM), and nuclear magnetic
resonance (NMR) would be valuable. Given the extreme structural sensitivity of the electronic
states demonstrated here, physical pressure or uniaxial strain experiments are highly warranted.
Such studies may reveal pressure-induced phase transitions, including the possible emergence
of  unconventional  superconductivity,  as  observed  in  related  vanadium-                 or  chromium-based
Kagome systems15,30. On the other hand, the study of heavy-fermion-like behavior in d-electron

materials  is  still  at  an  early  stage.  Quantum  oscillation  and  angle-resolved  photoemission
spectroscopy (ARPES) measurements would be invaluable to directly probe the effective mass
enhancement and detailed band structure, respectively. Furthermore, for Rb3V9Te13 analogue,
microscopic probes such as muon spin rotation/relaxation (ߤSR) and inelastic neutron scattering
(INS) will be essential to characterize the low-energy spin dynamics and resolve the nature of
its frustrated magnetic ground state. Finally, comprehensive theoretical studies focusing on the
flat-band physics and orbital-selective correlations of the Mosaic lattice are needed. Unraveling
how  the  unique  trimer-based  geometry  modulates  the  competition  between  localized  and
itinerant electronic degrees of freedom will be key to understanding the rich phase diagram of
this new family of materials.
4. Method
4.1 Single Crystal Growth

      Single  crystals  of  Cs3V9Te13       or  Rb3V9Te13      were  grown  using  a  flux  method.  Starting
materials, including Cs or Rb pieces (2N), V powder (3N), and Te lumps (5N) with a total mass
of 3 g, were weighed according to a molar ratio of (Cs or Rb) : V : Te = 7 : 3 : 12. The mixture
was then loaded into an alumina crucible, which was subsequently sealed in a tantalum tube
under an argon atmosphere. The tantalum tube was finally encapsulated in an evacuated quartz
                                                         12

ampoule. The assembly was heated to 1000 ℃ over 15 hours, held at this temperature for 24
hours, and then slowly cooled to 650 ℃ at a rate of 2 K/h. After the reaction, the excess Te flux
was  removed  by  high-temperature  centrifugation,  followed  by  mechanical  exfoliation  and
rinsing  with  deionized  water,  yielding  high-quality  single  crystals  with  clean  surfaces  for
subsequent  measurements.         The  polycrystalline  sample  was  synthesized  using  a  procedure
similar to that for the single crystal. Stoichiometric amounts of the constituent elements were
mixed according to the chemical formula, slowly heated to 900 °C, and annealed for 48 h to
obtain the polycrystalline powder. All sample handling procedures were performed inside an
argon-filled glovebox (H2O and O2 levels < 0.1 ppm).

4.2 Single Crystal X-ray Diffraction (SC-XRD)

      The single crystal X-ray diffraction (SC-XRD) was performed using a single crystal X-ray
diffractometer (D8 VENTURE, Bruker) under both 300 K and 100 K with a N2-based cryo
cooling system. The PHOTON III C14 detector captured graphite-monochromatized Mo Kα
radiation with a wavelength of 0.71073 Å. At 30 K, the SC-XRD pattern was collected using a
PHOTON II diffractometer with a multilayer mirror monochromatized Mo Kα radiation (λ =
0.71073 Å) and a He-based cryocooling system. APEX4 software was utilized to correct the
raw data. The ShelXT structure solution program was used to solve the structures using direct
methods, and the ShelXL least-squares refinement package in the Olex2 program was employed
to refine the structure31. The PLATON program's ADDSYM algorithm was applied to detect
any possible higher symmetry, but no missing symmetry was found for the above materials32.

The related crystallographic data are summarized in Tables S1-S4.

4.3 Powder X-ray diffraction (P-XRD)

      Powder  XRD  data  were  collected  at  room  temperature  on  a  Bruker  D8  Advance  Eco
diffractometer using Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) over a 2θ range of 10° to 90° (Figures S1
and S9).

4.4 X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS)

      XPS measurements were performed using a Thermo K-Alpha spectrometer. Samples were
prepared, cleaved, and handled entirely in an argon-filled glovebox to avoid air exposure, and
then transferred to the instrument under inert conditions.

4.5 Physical Property Measurement System (PPMS)

      Magnetic, transport, and thermal properties were characterized using a Quantum Design
PPMS.  DC  magnetic  susceptibilities          were   measured  on  single  crystals       of  Cs3V9Te13     and
                                                       13

Rb3V9Te13 from 1.8 to 300 K under various magnetic fields. Electrical resistivity within the ab
plane was determined by the standard four-probe method. The Hall coefficient was determined
by  sweeping  the  magnetic  field  from  positive  to  negative  at  a  fixed  temperature,  with  the
magnetoresistance component subtracted. Specific heat capacity was measured from 300 down
to 1.8 K using the relaxation technique. Measurements were extended to 0.06 K using a dilution
refrigerator attachment.

4.6 Theoretic simulations

      The  electronic  structure  of  Cs3V9Te13         was  calculated  using  density  functional  theory
(DFT)   as   implemented   in   the   Vienna   ab   initio   Simulation   Package   (VASP)33.   The

experimentally determined crystal structure was fully relaxed until the residual forces on each
atom were smaller than 5 meV/Å. Van der Waals interactions were included using the zero-
damping Grimme correction34 during structural optimization. The ground-state magnetic order

is identified as A-type antiferromagnetism, with the V spin moments aligned ferromagnetically
within the ab plane and antiferromagnetically along the out-of-plane direction. This magnetic
configuration  is  therefore  used  in  the  electronic  structure  calculations.               The   exchange–
correlation potential was treated within the generalized gradient approximation PBE35 in the
structure relaxation  and meta-generalized-gradient approximation SCAN36                      in band structure

calculation. A plane-wave energy cutoff of 300 eV was employed, and the Brillouin zone was
sampled using a 5×5×7 k-point mesh. The spin-orbit coupling is omitted throughout.
